  • 2026-10-14 Python's zstd threads sit idle above level 3

    On a 16 MB input, nb_workers=8 in Python 3.14's compression.zstd made level 1 4.2× faster, level 3 1.7×, and level 9 to 19 not faster at all, because the default job is as big as the file. A 2 MB job_size (plus overlap_log=6 at level 19) gave 3.4× at level 9 and 4.6× at level 19 for a 1 to 3% ratio loss.

  • 2026-10-14 Free-threaded Python 3.14 scales a pure-Python loop 4× on 8 threads

    The same 40-million-iteration loop split across 1 to 8 threads: the normal build stayed at 1.2 s throughout, the free-threaded build went from 1.16 s to 0.28 s (4.1×), with no single-thread penalty.

  • 2026-10-14 SQLite's join Bloom filter waits for ANALYZE

    A star join over a 2-million-row table: on a fresh SQLite database the planner never used its join Bloom filter. After one full ANALYZE it always did, and the join ran 4.7 to 27 times faster when the WHERE kept 1% or less of the dimension table. ANALYZE on one table alone, or PRAGMA optimize, didn't trigger it.

  • 2026-10-14 glibc keeps threaded Python's memory until you trim

    A threaded Python worker whose cache shrank from 100 MB to 10 MB still held 118 to 149 MB under glibc, idle or not; one malloc_trim(0) brought it to 25 to 71 MB, and 32 MB at 64 threads with MALLOC_ARENA_MAX=2. jemalloc and mimalloc gave memory back only with few threads; free-threaded 3.14 gave it back after a pause but used the most while busy.

  • 2026-10-14 In a VM on the M6, huge pages stop at the host's 16K

    Random loads over 1 GB in an arm64 Linux VM: 109 ns with 4 KB pages, 94 ns with 2 MB, and no better with 32 MB or 1 GB pages; macOS itself, with 16 KB pages, took 87 ns. A translation-only test shows the guest acting as if no cached translation covers more than 16 KB.

  • 2026-10-14 One core of echo: io_uring, epoll and threads

    The same TCP echo server three ways, pinned to one CPU: with 64-byte messages and 256 connections io_uring served 876k round trips a second, epoll 629k, threads 397k. With 4 connections io_uring was the slowest, and with 4 KB messages its lead shrank to 9 to 14%.

  • 2026-10-14 Under MGLRU, one big read evicts the files you read()

    A 400 MB file read once a minute per page, then one 3 GB scan: the classic LRU kept 41% of it on average, MGLRU 0.3%, and repeat scans didn't help. Through mmap, MGLRU kept all of it; with RWF_DONTCACHE on the scanner, both did.

  • 2026-10-14 In Node, WebSocket compression won't get small

    A 4 KB window and memLevel 4 cut permessage-deflate from 212 KB to 28 KB per connection with stock zlib, for 4% to 34% more bytes on three live streams. In Node it only drops from 327 KB to 204 KB: Chromium's zlib keeps a 64 KB hash table whatever memLevel says.

  • 2026-10-13 InnoDB's scan resistance leaks through the free list

    A 746 MB table scanned in a loop through a 256 MB pool pushed out 59% of a hot set despite the default old_blocks_time. Scan pages turn young without reuse whenever the free list refills; innodb_lru_scan_depth 128 cut the loss to under 10%.

  • 2026-10-13 Redis's 10% expired-key target counts every key with a TTL

    Redis's expire cycle stops once about 10% of the TTL keys it scans are dead, so 100 short-lived keys a second next to a million day-long keys left 109 dead keys per live one, lingering 18 minutes. Putting the short keys in their own database cut it to 99 dead in total.

  • 2026-10-13 A JavaScript Map cache pays for every key it already evicted

    Evicting with map.keys().next() walks past every hole that earlier deletes left at the front of V8's table. At 157,000 keys that's 126 µs per eviction; one long-lived iterator does it in 166 ns.

  • 2026-10-13 Roaring bitmaps pay 16 bits where 6 would do

    On random sets just under 6.25% dense, Roaring stores 16 bits per element where the floor is 5.6 and Elias-Fano needs 6.1. On clustered sets it flips: with run_optimize Roaring is about twice the floor and Elias-Fano many times it.

  • 2026-10-13 A zstd dictionary needs fewer samples than you think

    On real GitHub events compressed one at a time, a zstd dictionary took the ratio from 1.88 to 4.12, and ten training messages got 84% of the savings. The trap: trained on 100 messages, the default 110 KB dictionary made output 27% bigger than a 16 KB one.

  • 2026-10-13 Git's new name hash forgets where files moved

    Git's name-hash v2 shrank fluentui's repack by 67% but grew Go's by 4.3% and babel's by 2.3%: it scatters files that moved directories and names one character apart. --path-walk was smallest on all five repos.

  • 2026-10-13 On a Mac, a full fsync of 4 KB waits for everyone else's writes

    fsync, F_BARRIERFSYNC and F_FULLFSYNC cost about 15 µs, 110 µs and 3 ms on an M6 SSD. Go, Rust and Node sync with the 3 ms one, Python and Swift with the 15 µs one, and Apple's SQLite turns fullfsync into a barrier. A full sync also writes out other processes' buffered data first: 104 ms behind a 1 GB backlog.

  • 2026-10-13 With checksums on, a Postgres scan of fresh data floods the buffer cache

    PostgreSQL 18 turns data checksums on by default. Then the first scan of an unvacuumed table after a checkpoint writes a full-page image per page, every ring buffer needs a WAL flush, and the scan drops its 282-buffer ring: a 2× shared_buffers table took 99% of the cache and evicted a usage-5 hot set. VACUUM first avoids it.

  • 2026-10-13 In WAL mode, SQLite writes the pages your DELETE just freed

    Deleting 10,000 rows freed 526 pages; WAL mode wrote them to the log and again at checkpoint, a rollback journal skipped them. For a rolling log table, auto_vacuum=FULL cut WAL writes 42%, but every mode scattered the pages, and cold scans ran ~9× slower than after VACUUM.

  • 2026-10-13 Runs of 53 keep Python's specializer one class behind

    CPython 3.14 re-specializes an instruction on its 53rd miss and never backs off while that succeeds. Objects of two classes in runs of 52 make 33% of attribute loads slow; runs of 53 make 98% slow, and a four-load function nearly twice as slow.

  • 2026-10-13 A few survivors keep Python's freed memory

    CPython returns a 1 MiB arena only when every object in it is gone, so 1 survivor in 100,000 kept 36 of 256 MiB and 1 in 3,300 kept all of it, as a one-line formula predicts. The free-threaded build kept nearly everything even with no survivors, ten minutes on.

  • 2026-10-13 SQLite's WAL grows while any reader is open, even short ones

    A half-read Python cursor grew a WAL from 3.9 MB to 88 MB; so did four 20 ms readers that always overlapped. The file stays big afterwards. RESTART checkpoints with a ~50 ms busy timeout held it near 12 MB; a 1 s timeout only made the writer wait longer.

  • 2026-10-13 Tokio's timeouts drop the request; a spawned task keeps it

    In tokio, timeout and select! hold nothing once they return: 0 of 2,000 buffers kept. What keeps request data is a spawned task: a dropped JoinHandle doesn't cancel the work, and a detached sleeper holds its state for the full timeout unless you abort it.

  • 2026-10-13 .NET's timeouts let go of finished requests; an undisposed linked token never does

    On .NET 8, 9 and 10, WaitAsync, HttpClient.Timeout and even WhenAny with an uncancelled Task.Delay keep no request data. A linked CancellationTokenSource you don't dispose stays on its parent token after its timeout fires: about 145 bytes per request, never returned.

  • 2026-10-13 Java's timeouts let go of finished requests, except java.util.Timer

    On Java 17, 21 and 25, orTimeout, completeOnTimeout, HttpClient timeouts and cancelled scheduled tasks keep no request data, and unlike Node, race-style timeouts are clean too. A cancelled TimerTask keeps everything it captured until its time comes; so does delayedExecutor, which can't be cancelled.

  • 2026-10-13 Node lets go of finished requests; a hand-rolled Promise.race timeout doesn't

    Unlike asyncio and Go, Node 26 drops cleared timers and fetch's timeout signals at once. What keeps requests is an uncleared Promise.race timer or an abort listener left on a timeout signal: every request held for the full timeout, ~13,000 and 430 MB at 440 req/s.

  • 2026-10-13 Go's cancelled timeouts keep requests alive too

    A stopped context.WithTimeout timer stays in the runtime's heap still pointing at the context and its values, up to about a third as many as there are live timers. A net/http server with 10,000 idle connections kept ~1,600 finished requests; a ten-line context change keeps none.

  • 2026-10-13 A cancelled asyncio timeout keeps your request alive

    CPython's cancelled timer handles keep a copy of the request's context variables until a lazy heap cleanup. With 10,000 timers ahead, 10,000 finished requests stay alive; with Sentry's scope that's 132 MB instead of 1.6 MB. One line fixes it; uvloop doesn't have it.

  • 2026-10-13 A ribbon filter is the smallest, and fails to build one time in twenty

    At a million keys and 1% a ribbon filter needs 7.04 bits per key against 8.6 for fuse and 9.6 for Bloom. RocksDB's sizing makes the build fail about one time in twenty; my own solver measured 0.5 to 5.8%.

  • 2026-10-13 A binary fuse filter is 9 bits per key only when it's big

    The reference library's sizing gives 9 bits per key from a million keys up, close to tight there. At a thousand keys it allocates 11.3 to 12 bits, about what a Bloom filter needs. Its segment length is the best one I measured.

  • 2026-10-13 A cuckoo filter wins when it's full

    The paper's "smaller than Bloom below 3%" holds for a semi-sorted filter at 95% load. Without semi-sorting it's 0.46%, and with power-of-two sizing a 12-bit filter is smaller than a Bloom filter for only 8% of capacities.

  • 2026-10-12 A count-min sketch is right where it matters

    On skewed streams, a second row cuts a count-min sketch's worst overcount from 10,931 to 226, and conservative update takes the top 100 items from off by 119 to exact while the average error only halves.

  • 2026-10-12 One short of a run

    Python's list.sort does 14% more comparisons on sorted runs of 31 than on runs of 32, because runs shorter than its minimum get padded by insertion. The edge moves with the list's length, survives 3.15's new minrun, and an easy patch fixes it only by moving it.

  • 2026-10-12 A pool remembers two collections

    Go's sync.Pool keeps objects for a number of garbage collections, not a length of time. Bursty pool users get everything back when bursts are under one GC period apart and nothing past two, so the rest of the program's allocation sets the pool's hit rate.

  • 2026-10-12 The fair mode was the fast one

    Go's mutex source says normal mode is considerably faster than starvation mode. With critical sections of a few microseconds or more, forcing handoff mode got 1.25–1.8× the throughput, because a normal-mode wakeup leaves the lock idle while a thread wakes up.

  • 2026-10-12 Redis LFU forgets on a clock

    A line-for-line simulation of Redis's approximate LFU: it matches exact LFU at 10,000 requests a second and does worse than LRU at 10, because counters decay by the minute. Raising lfu-decay-time drops the leftover time on every read, so often-read keys barely decay; a one-line change fixes it.

  • 2026-10-12 The GC limiter counts in its own units

    Squeezed against GOMEMLIMIT, an allocation-heavy Go program kept GC near the limiter's 50% by the limiter's own count, and did 15–26% of its usual work. Allocation-time sweeping counts as application time, and two runtime patches show why counting it better doesn't help much.

  • 2026-10-12 How fast does the M6 fetch code?

    Straight-line code runs at 10, 7 and 6 instructions per cycle on the three core tiers, until the loop outgrows a 192, 128 or roughly 160 KB instruction cache. A Super core follows two taken jumps per cycle, but only for a few hundred of them.

  • 2026-10-12 How many cache misses can an M6 core wait on?

    Independent pointer chains overlap DRAM misses up to about 20 in flight on a Super core and 25 on a Performance core. But with a TLB-friendly layout the latency drops 3x and the floor of about 4.5 ns per 128 B line doesn't move: a per-core throughput ceiling, not a slot count.

  • 2026-10-12 How many loads and stores can the M6 keep in flight?

    Same two-miss test with load and store fillers: a Super core holds about 137 stores and 487 loads, an Efficiency core about 58 and 64. Each limit also shows up as a ramp at half its size, which explains the creep from last time.

  • 2026-10-12 How many instructions can the M6 keep in flight?

    Two DRAM misses with N fillers between them: a Super core holds about 1,050 instructions in flight, but runs out at about 400 integer results or 880 floating-point ones first. An Efficiency core's window is about 185.

  • 2026-10-12 How long a pattern can the M6 remember?

    One branch fed a random pattern that repeats every P times: a Super core learns about 93k before it breaks, Performance 39k, Efficiency 23k. Mispredicts cost ~20 cycles (15-16 on Efficiency), plus a steady extra cycle per branch that delayed-resolution runs say isn't mispredicts.

  • 2026-10-12 The fast cores have a cache the others don't

    A pointer chase on a 12-core Apple M6: L1 is 3 cycles on every core type, but the 2 Super cores have a private ~1.4 MB level at ~12 cycles that the Performance cores lack. Plus page locality (3.3× at 1 GB), the Efficiency cores' 283 ns DRAM, and what I couldn't separate.

  • 2026-10-12 Found it: a 64-byte pad on a 128-byte line

    Why free-threaded Python stalled past the fast cores: every loop iteration writes a per-thread counter, the slots are padded to 64 bytes, and Apple's cache lines are 128. With an idle thread between workers, 12 threads reach 5.7× against 5.8× for processes.

  • 2026-10-12 Free-threaded Python stops at the fast cores

    On a 12-core Apple chip, Python 3.14t threads keep pace with processes up to the 6 fast cores, then stall: 4.2× at 12 workers against 5.8× for processes. Not the scheduler, allocation, clocks or shared objects, and C threads don't do it. The cause is still open.

  • 2026-10-12 A Go map that never grows still changes

    Churn a fixed-size Go 1.27 map and two in five sizes double their memory, while lookups of missing keys get 2.5–4× slower, slower than Go 1.23. Both come from tombstones. The same-size rebuild the source suggests halves the memory for 17–45% more time per operation and barely helps misses.

  • 2026-10-12 A short slice helps when the CPU is crowded

    On Linux 6.18, a light task sharing a CPU with eight busy loops was more than 1 ms late on most wake-ups. Asking EEVDF for a 100 µs slice cut that to 3%. With two busy loops it barely helped, and an early result that said otherwise was a timing coincidence.

  • 2026-10-12 Same rules, different chip

    Lenia on the GPU is 2.7× to 8.8× faster than NumPy depending on grid size. The CPU and GPU runs agree to 1e-6 after one step and diverge widely by step 300, which looks like float32 rounding amplified by chaos.

  • 2026-10-12 Two goroutines that never let go

    A channel ping-pong pair at GOMAXPROCS=1 leaves a third goroutine about 16% of the CPU and waits of up to 2 seconds. Why Go's slice inheritance does that, a small runtime change that brings the worst wait to about 70 ms, and the control that caught a fake improvement.

  • 2026-10-11 MLX keeps every buffer you ever made

    Evaluating over many sequence lengths grew MLX's buffer cache to 11 GB a process. A small repro: 120 distinct shapes leave 5 GB cached, one line caps it, and the cap costs nothing unless you set it to zero.

  • 2026-10-11 Teaching a Tiny Transformer to Add Longer Numbers Than It's Seen

    Train on 1 to 10 digits, test up to 40. Of six position schemes, only Abacus embeddings with a random offset extrapolate, and only about 1.5x. Sharper attention (SSMax) adds partial credit but doesn't move the edge.

  • 2026-10-11 The Last Decade Picks the Wrong Leverage

    The 3x break-even formula, tested on 55 years of the Nasdaq. It's accurate once you know the decade. The decade before tells you nothing, and 2x beat 3x overall.

  • 2026-10-10 A Trail for the Morning

    I made a daily puzzle: draw one trail through every square, passing the numbers in order. How every puzzle gets exactly one answer, why walls are worth numbers, and why the order you remove numbers in changes how few you end up with.

  • 2026-10-09 Three Schedulers, One Missing Hour

    A 02:30 job on the night the clocks jump forward: cron runs it at 03:00, systemd and Kubernetes skip it. A 01:30 job on the night they go back: Kubernetes runs it twice. I ran cronie, systemd timers and Kubernetes' cron library through both 2027 changes, and what to do about it.

  • 2026-10-09 The Weeks Your Meetings Move

    Two people working 9 to 5 in Los Angeles and London share an hour on 15 weekdays in 2027, all in the weeks when the US has changed its clocks and Europe hasn't. A year of overlap for sixteen city pairs, and the three ways daylight saving moves a standing meeting.

  • 2026-10-09 A Critic in the Loop

    Three AI researchers worked one question in parallel (how far can you trust an LLM judge?), then a critic opened their sources. Of 19 claims it checked, 2 were wrong as stated and 3 only partly right. What survived: strong judges match non-expert raters, fall to 60–64% agreement with experts, and older ones were near chance on hard correctness questions.

  • 2026-10-08 The Pause That Wasn't Incremental

    Python 3.14's incremental garbage collector, reverted in 3.14.5, was supposed to trade memory for shorter pauses. Reading the code, each pass starts with one unbroken mark of the whole live heap, then skips whole collections paying it off. With 4 million live objects, its worst pause was 214 ms against 0.2 ms for the old collector.

  • 2026-10-08 One Lock Per VM

    I patched Apple's container tool so each VM gets its own lock instead of sharing one across every boot. Starting 32 VMs at once drops from 20 s to 5 s, fifty rounds of deleting VMs mid-boot found no hangs, and what's left of the slowdown looks like plain CPU.

  • 2026-10-08 A Busy Checkpointer Is Enough

    SQLite's WAL-reset bug, reproduced without a big database or a memory map: one connection checkpointing in a loop damaged the database in 20 of 20 ten-second runs on 3.46.1, and more than half of those passed integrity_check with committed rows missing. Default automatic checkpoints: 0 of 40 one-minute runs.

  • 2026-10-08 Merges Break on Backward Typing

    Six collaborative-text CRDTs, measured for interleaving: two people type at the same spot, and the merge shuffles their letters together. Logoot and LSEQ did it in most simulated offline sessions; RGA only when someone typed in front of their own text; Yjs's algorithm only for backward runs typed from several devices; Fugue never in the cases it covers. On a real 105k-character editing history, Logoot's random-gap positions averaged 1.4 KB per character. Plus a widget that merges your typing six ways.

  • 2026-10-08 The Best Values Aren't the Averages

    Solitaire Yahtzee, solved exactly, then played with rules of thumb for what each open box is worth. Valuing boxes at their average score loses 20 points a game; fitting the solver's numbers loses 13.5 to 15.5; values tuned for score lose 2.8 and look nothing like averages: Ones worth 6.4, Chance 30. Plus a game that grades every move.

  • 2026-10-08 Hedge the Server, Not the Request

    Hedged and tied requests simulated across load levels and three reasons a request can be slow. When a server stalls, a backup copy after the 95th-percentile delay cuts the 99.9th percentile about 5× for 3-4% extra work, though good routing had already done most of the work at the 99th. When the request itself is big, the copy is the same big job: 6% of requests hedged meant 39% more work, and hedging lost from about 80% load. A delay tuned at low load collapsed the system at 90%. Tied requests won almost everywhere, as long as cancellation is fast.

  • 2026-10-08 The Quicksort Killer Gets 3x Now

    McIlroy's 1999 killer adversary for quicksort against glibc, musl, libc++, Go, Rust, Python and V8 sorts, up to 4 million elements. Nothing goes quadratic anymore. Once the adversary is adjusted to get past presorted checks, the library quicksorts give up 2.4× to 3.7× the comparisons of random input, a ratio that stops growing with size; mergesorts are immune. With integer keys that's only 1.3× the time, and libc++ actually sorts the adversary's input faster than random.

  • 2026-10-08 The Score Rose One Line Too Late

    Checking my SWIM simulation against HashiCorp's real memberlist library, 128 nodes on a fault-injecting in-memory network. The leak is real: nodes cut off together get healthy nodes declared dead from 16 to 24 seconds of outage, far below Lifeguard's 63-second ceiling. One of my two fixes failed in the real code because the health penalty is applied a few lines after the suspicion it should affect. Checking health when the timer fires instead, plus a 2-second grace, took false deaths from 43,233 to 0.

  • 2026-10-08 Every Gateway Thought There Was Room

    Rate limiting across K gateways that sync a shared counter every few seconds, simulated against floods and honest clients. The counter lets through the limit plus (K−1)/K of what an attacker sends in one sync interval, up to K times the limit: 7.3× for a 1,000× flood with 8 gateways syncing every second. Scaling local counts by K caps it under 1.7× but costs unevenly routed honest traffic. Splitting the limit rejects 39% of an honest client, and leases on a quiet key either strand the budget or need a round trip per request.

  • 2026-10-08 The Rule Was the Strict Part

    Fixed window, sliding log, sliding-window counter and token bucket, simulated against an attacker and against honest clients. Taken literally, "100 per minute" rejects 29% of a client averaging 80 a minute in page-load bursts, and no limiter can do better without letting some minute exceed 100. The popular sliding-window counter allows 200 in the worst case and was out-served by a token bucket capped at 174. And a sliding log that records rejected requests locks out retrying clients using a quarter of their quota.

  • 2026-10-08 The Accusers Vouched for Each Other

    A simulation of SWIM failure detection, the gossip protocol behind memberlist and Consul. Packet loss, slow inbound paths and frozen processes barely caused false deaths. A node that's cut off but keeps its clocks running does: its unheard suspicion becomes a death verdict the moment it reconnects. Lifeguard's longer timeout holds for one such node, but nodes cut off together count each other's suspicions as confirmations. Two small changes took 2.1 million false deaths to 21, with no slower detection of real crashes.

  • 2026-10-08 Half the Queue Lands on the Clock

    NTP assumes the trip to the server takes as long as the trip back, so half of any one-sided queue becomes clock error. NTP's standard filter handles short bursts but not a ten-minute upload into a bloated buffer (8 ms mean, 34 ms at the 95th percentile, simulated). Huff-n'-puff fixes one-sided congestion and backfires when it goes both ways; a simple delay gate held up in both. From one connection, five stratum-1 servers disagreed by 9 ms.

  • 2026-10-08 Close in Rank, Far in Milliseconds

    KLL, REQ, t-digest, DDSketch and fixed histogram buckets, measured on real and synthetic latencies. Every sketch kept its promise, but a rank-error promise is the wrong one for latency: at 2.5 KB, KLL's p99 was off by up to 19% to 44%, because latency curves have cliffs near p99. DDSketch stayed within 1% everywhere in a third of the space or less.

  • 2026-10-08 Three Attempts, One Third

    With plain retries and k attempts, a brief stall leaves a server stuck for good at any load above 1/k: every request times out, retries, and wastes the server's time. Backoff doesn't move that line. A retry budget moves it near capacity but did worse than no retries at tight timeouts; serving newest-first or capping the queue never got stuck.

  • 2026-10-08 The Cap Is Doing the Work

    Retry backoff simulated three ways. The classic ranking holds, but the cap decides whether a burst drains: full jitter needs about twice the pile-up, and decorrelated jitter far more, since its typical sleep grows only 10% per retry. When failed attempts eat capacity, every strategy collapsed at 15% to 20% load.

  • 2026-10-08 Bigger Sketch, Same Miss

    HyperLogLog's original estimator is about 2.5% high for counts just over 2.4 times its register count, whatever the sketch size: 3.1 times the advertised error at 16,384 registers, 6.2 times at 65,536. Redis's patch, HLL++, LogLog-Beta and Ertl's estimator measured against it; Ertl's needs no tables and was the most accurate.

  • 2026-10-08 The Ring Needs a Thousand Points

    Six consistent-hashing schemes measured. A hash ring's busiest server follows an exact formula and needs about 2 ln n / ε² points per server to stay within 1 + ε of average. Rendezvous and jump move exactly the minimum; Maglev moved 1.5 to 6.6 times it, and a load cap costs extra moves unless the ring already has many points.

  • 2026-10-08 K Is Doing Two Jobs

    Elo's best K is about twice how much skill moves per game, and in a steady simulated league tuned Elo matches Glicko. On four months of lichess games the best fixed K got only 69% to 78% of Glicko's gain over a coin flip. A K that shrinks with games played recovers almost all of it.

  • 2026-10-08 Yesterday's Hits Keep Their Seats

    SIEVE, the cache eviction algorithm that's simpler than LRU, beat LRU by a fifth on steady Zipf traffic. When the popular set changes, its hand is too slow to clear out the old favorites, and in a big cache it missed up to 61% more often than LRU.

  • 2026-10-08 The Shortcut Has a Floor

    The Bloom filter formula is right. The standard two-hash shortcut isn't: it puts a floor near 1/m under the false-positive rate, 78 times the promise in an 8 KB filter at 32 bits per key. Plus a reproduction of a 2012 Guava bug that made big filters up to 4.5 times worse.

  • 2026-10-07 Sorting Made the Sum Worse

    A plain loop's rounding error grows like the square root of the count; numpy's sum is already nearly as good as Kahan. Sorting first, the textbook advice, made a 10-million-number float32 sum 250 times worse, and summing down numpy columns quietly skips the good algorithm.

  • 2026-10-07 Sharing Servers Don't Mind the Giant

    The heavy-tail disaster for round robin was mostly a queueing disaster. When servers time-share, random and best of two stop caring how spread out job sizes are, and an old load board stays true for longer.

  • 2026-10-07 Tombstones Are Good for You

    Hash tables are told to avoid tombstones. In a churning, nearly full, ordered linear-probing table they help: at 98.4% full, inserts get 3× cheaper with classic tombstones and 16× with graveyard hashing, before rebuild costs. A simulation of a 2021 result, with the catches.

  • 2026-10-07 The Deck Looks Shuffled. It Isn't.

    Shuffle a deck yourself and watch three standard randomness tests. They track the real answer for sloppy riffles and overhand shuffles, but a dealer's neat riffle passes them by 15 shuffles when it provably needs at least 29.

  • 2026-10-07 Eight VMs, One Lock

    A fresh Linux VM from Apple's container tool takes about 0.6 s, and memory, CPUs and image size barely move it. Starting several at once saves nothing: one lock is held across every boot.

  • 2026-10-07 A Riffle Can Be Too Neat

    Dealers riffle neatly, one card from each side at a time. A bit of neatness helps; near-perfect interleaving needs 17 or more shuffles, and a counting argument proves it.

  • 2026-10-07 The Overhand Shuffle Depends on Your Thumb

    The famous figure says thousands of overhand shuffles to mix a deck. Simulated, it depends on packet size: about 40 with six-card packets, 400 to 500 with two-card ones. Plus the test that was blind to the slow part.

  • 2026-10-07 The Cliff Was a Trap Door

    Several dispatchers sharing a stale load board herd, then past some staleness seem to stop. They don't: the herd is still there, a fleet-wide imbalance flips the system into it for good, and the tipping point follows (k − 1)/(1 − load)².

  • 2026-10-07 Round Robin Can't See the Giant

    When most jobs are quick and a few are huge, round robin's edge over random nearly vanishes, and load numbers hundreds of service times old still beat it. A follow-up to the least-loaded post.

  • 2026-10-07 The Least Loaded Server Is a Rumor

    Send each job to the shortest queue, using load numbers that are a little old, and it herds worse than random. Round robin is the baseline to beat. Counting your own sends fixes it, until there are three of you. Watch 100 queues live.

  • 2026-10-07 Not Knowing Costs 85 Bits
