Tools

Small web tools I built and keep running. Each one is a form in the browser, plain text for curl, and JSON at /api. No accounts, no tracking, nothing stored.

Fox Trail

A daily path puzzle for your phone. Draw one trail that fills every square, passes the numbers in order and stays off the walls. Three new ones at midnight where you are, each with exactly one answer. How they're made: A Trail for the Morning.

cron, explained

Paste a cron line and a time zone. You get what it means in English and its next runs, with daylight-saving gaps and repeats handled the way systemd timers handle them. Below the list, a table shows the next two clock changes and where cronie and Kubernetes CronJob would run the line differently. The next run list reaches far enough to show a February 29 if your line hits one.

Try this: 30 2 * * 0 in New York, which runs at 02:30 every Sunday, except the spring Sunday when clocks jump from 02:00 to 03:00 and 02:30 never happens. On that night cronie runs it at 03:00 instead. Or from a terminal:

curl 'https://cron.wickkit.cc/?e=0+9+*+*+1-5&tz=Europe/London'

Each result also links a calendar file with the line's next 50 runs as events, so a schedule shows up next to everything else you have on. Name the events and set their length: a nightly 03:00 Berlin backup. Subscribe to its address and it keeps rolling forward.

Leveraged fund drag

Does a 2x or 3x daily-reset fund beat its index? Put in your guesses for the index's return and volatility, the cash rate, the fund's fee and how long you hold. It shows the typical growth against the index, the volatility drag, the break-even return, the leverage that maximizes typical growth, and the odds of beating the index, with a chart of growth against leverage.

Try this: a 3x fund on an index returning 10% at 20% volatility. The average outcome looks great; the typical one trails the index. The math behind it is in The break-even line for 3x.

Meeting times across time zones

List two to six time zones and everyone's working hours (each person can have their own). It finds when everyone is at work at once, day by day for up to twelve weeks or a whole calendar year, and groups the days into stretches with the same meeting times. When a stretch starts because one country changed its clocks, it says which one.

Try this: London and Chicago in March 2027. The usual two shared hours become three for the two weeks between the US changing its clocks on March 14 and the UK on March 28. Or from a terminal:

curl 'https://tz.wickkit.cc/?z=Europe/Berlin&z=America/New_York&z=America/Sao_Paulo'

Every result also links a calendar file: one all-day event on each day the shared hours change, saying the new times and why. London and Chicago, 2027 has five: the usual two hours on January 1, three hours from March 15 and November 1, and back to two from March 29 and November 8. Import it once, or subscribe to its address so it stays current.

Filter sizer

How many bits does a Bloom, blocked Bloom, cuckoo, xor or binary fuse filter need for your number of keys and false-positive rate? It sizes each one the way its reference library allocates it (power-of-two cuckoo tables, the fuse filter's size factor for small sets, the fingerprint widths the libraries actually ship), next to the lower bound, and warns where my measurements found trouble.

Try this: a million keys at 1%, where a binary fuse filter beats Bloom, 9.0 bits per key to 9.6. Then 3,000 keys at 0.1%, where plain Bloom wins by a wide margin: the fuse filter pays its small-set penalty and has to use 16-bit fingerprints. The background is in A cuckoo filter wins when it's full and A binary fuse filter is 9 bits per key only when it's big.

Should I use a zstd dictionary?

Paste a few hundred of your own messages (log lines, JSON events, API responses). It trains zstd dictionaries from 1 KB to 110 KB on most of them, compresses the rest one at a time with and without each, and shows the ratio, how many messages each dictionary needs to pay for itself, and how close it gets to compressing everything as one stream. Then download the dictionary it picked. It's libzstd 1.5.7 compiled to WebAssembly, so your messages never leave the browser.

The built-in example, a made-up API access log of ~260-byte JSON lines, goes from 1.17× alone to 4.68× with a 2 KB dictionary. Background: A zstd dictionary needs fewer samples than you think.

Riffle calculator

How much can a card guesser win after k riffles of a deck of n cards? The guesser knows the shuffle and sees each card as it's dealt, and the calculator plays the best one possible against a random guess over the cards still unseen. It shows the extra cards it gets right over a whole deal, with a standard error from simulated deals. Background: Past six riffles, each shuffle halves what a card guesser can win.

Gray-Scott playground

Two chemicals on a grid, running in your browser. Move the feed rate F and the kill rate k and watch spots split, turn into mazes, coral or worms. Presets for the usual regions, and drag on the grid to drop more of the chemical. Background: Mazes Don't Finish.

Sketch sizer: HyperLogLog and count-min

How many registers does a HyperLogLog need, and how deep should a count-min sketch be? Give it the range of counts you expect and a target error, and it shows, for every register count from 16 to 262,144, the memory and the worst error over your whole range, from 10,000 simulated runs per count rather than the textbook 1.04/√m. For count-min, give it the stream, the number of keys, how skewed they are and the memory, and it runs the sketch on a simulated stream in your browser at each depth.

Try the original HyperLogLog estimator from a thousand to a hundred million: no register count gets it under 1%, because its bias near 2.5 times the register count stays at about 2.5% however big the sketch gets. With a bias-corrected estimator, 12 KB gets 0.81%. In the count-min example, 32 KB on a skewed stream of half a million items, depth 2 averages 29 over and depth 8 averages 98. Background: Bigger sketch, same miss and A count-min sketch is right where it matters.
